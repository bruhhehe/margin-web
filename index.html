It reads the page you already have open.

In your own browser, signed in as you, in a hidden tab that never takes your focus. No API, and nothing sent to a server to be scraped — a store serves a captcha to datacentre traffic anyway. Twenty-two Amazon sites and AliExpress today, the same fields out of every one.

Paste a list, or point it at a page

Links however they come, bare product codes, or a column off a spreadsheet. Or open a search, a best-seller chart, a category or a storefront and take every product off it — and off the pages after it. You trim the list before anything is prepared.

Nothing goes live until you have seen it

Four things the extension adds that this calculator leaves out.

  • A price floor. It will not list below it, whatever the maths says.
  • Rounding that only goes up. So tidying the price never eats the profit.
  • A profit floor. A re-price that would lose money is blocked, not published.
  • Your own percentage. For Promoted Listings and anything else eBay takes off the same sale.

You can also replace any rate with the percentage and fixed fee off your own eBay invoice, which beats any published table.

Margin keeps checking the product page after you list.

Every 15 minutes, or every few hours — you choose. You also set how small a price move it should ignore. Each of the three below is its own switch, and they all start off.

  1. The store changes the price

    Margin works out a new eBay price from the new cost, using the same profit rule and that listing’s own category. If the new price would leave you less than your profit floor, it does not send it — it flags the listing and leaves the old price alone.

  2. The store runs out of stock

    Margin sets your eBay quantity to zero, and puts it back when the store has stock again.

  3. Your eBay listing sells out

    If eBay is holding a listing at zero because it sold, and the store still has stock, Margin puts it back up at the quantity you first published.

Notifications name the product rather than its product code, and forty price changes arrive as one notification rather than forty.

Then there is the part that happens every day.

A sale is a thing to buy, a parcel to post and a tracking number to send back. Orders is that list, with the count on the toolbar icon so a sale is visible with the panel shut.

  1. What is due, and when

    Everything waiting, overdue first and in red, with due-today called out. The buyer’s address reads as a label — town, county, postcode, country written out — and copies in one click.

  2. Buy it without retyping anything

    Opens the product page with the quantity already set and fills the buyer’s address into the checkout when it appears. A bar says which order the tab is for. Nothing presses Buy now.

  3. Tracking, back to eBay

    The parcel number is read off the order page and the carrier worked out from its shape — ten are recognised by name. Send one, or every order you have bought, in one go.

Two things to know before you start.

  • You buy the stock and you post it.

    Margin handles the listing, the price and the watching. When an order comes in, “Order on Amazon” opens the product page with the quantity set and fills in the buyer’s address — then you press Buy now, and you post the parcel.

  • Your shop data stays yours.

    No analytics and no third-party calls. Nothing leaves your browser except to Margin and to eBay, and your eBay tokens are encrypted at rest rather than held in the extension.

Questions people ask first.

Is this allowed under eBay’s dropshipping rules?

eBay allows dropshipping if you fulfil orders from a wholesale supplier, but not if another retailer posts the item straight to your buyer. Margin is built for the first kind of seller: you hold the stock and you post it. Worth reading eBay’s own wording before you list.

Is there anything to set up?

No. Install it, create an account, connect your eBay shop, and list. Margin runs the server side; you do not deploy anything and there is nothing to configure.

Your eBay refresh token is encrypted and held on that server rather than in the extension, because a browser extension is the wrong place to keep the key to your shop.

How accurate is the fee?

It is an estimate, and it has to be: eBay only works out the real fee once the sale exists, and tells you afterwards. But you have to price the listing before that, so this is the best figure to price against.

Rates come from each site’s published table, per category, in all four shapes eBay uses, with the regulatory operating fee included. eBay UK’s is checked against real order statements — which is why you can add the extra percentage an invoice shows on top, for Promoted Listings and anything else eBay takes without publishing a rate. After a sale, the Orders tab shows what it actually charged.

Which eBay sites does it work on?

Fourteen: the United Kingdom, United States, Ireland, Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, Switzerland, Poland, Canada and Australia. Each has its own currency, fee table and site id.

Business and private sellers pay different fees, so Margin asks eBay and remembers the answer for a day. A private seller on eBay UK pays no final value fee; on eBay US they pay what a business pays. If eBay will not say, Margin assumes business — quoting no fee when you will be charged one is the expensive way to be wrong.

Does it use Amazon’s API?

No — neither the Product Advertising API nor the Creators API. Margin reads pages in your own browser, signed in as you. That is also the only approach that works: Amazon serves a captcha to a datacentre address instead of a product page.
