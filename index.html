Every listing priced from what it leaves you.

Margin is a Chrome side panel. Opened on an Amazon or AliExpress product, it already holds the title, the pictures, the specifications and the variations. It solves the eBay price from the amount you intend to be left with — using eBay’s published fee for your site, your category and your account type — and publishes to your own eBay account.

Margin reads the product page you already have open.

No Amazon API and no server-side scraping: pages are fetched and parsed inside your own browser, under your own session, by a page nobody sees — so nothing steals focus and nothing is read from a datacentre that Amazon would serve a captcha instead. Twenty-two Amazon marketplaces and AliExpress come out in the same shape.

Rows are read three at a time, each says which step it is on, a product already on eBay is marked and left out, and the batch survives the panel being closed.

Amazon is asked politely. Three requests at a time, 250 ms apart, whatever else the tool is doing. A price more than 20% away from the last one costs a slower, fuller read before it is believed.

The fee is in the price before you publish, not after the sale.

State what you want to keep and the listed price is solved so that eBay’s cut on the order total still leaves it. The rate is read off eBay’s published table for your site and category, the price is solved, the rate is read again at that price and the price re-solved — so a sale landing the other side of a fee threshold is priced on the right rate.

target = cost + what you want to keep + your own fixed costs price = (target + eBay’s fixed fee) ÷ (1 − eBay’s rate)

eBay charges by category, so this figure is only as good as the category assumed above. The extension uses the category you are actually listing in, and the rate it reads is the one for that category.

Rules the solver leaves out and the extension does not: a price floor, a rounding rule that only ever nudges upwards so the margin holds, a profit floor that refuses a repricing rather than publishing it at a loss, and an extra percentage for Promoted Listings and anything else off the same sale that eBay does not publish. Every rate can be replaced by the percentage and fixed fee from your own invoice, which is better evidence than any table.

The source page is then checked on a schedule.

Every fifteen minutes to every few hours, your choice, with a tolerance below which a price move is ignored. What it does about a change is three switches, and all three are off by default — a listing that changes its own price is a listing you have to trust.

  1. The source price moves

    The eBay price is re-solved from your rule on the new cost, with that listing’s own category feeding the fee. A repricing that would leave less than your profit floor is refused, the price is not sent, and the row is flagged.

  2. The source runs out

    Quantity to zero, and back to what it was when the source returns. A price that has merely vanished from the page is not a price of zero and is not acted on.

  3. It sold out on eBay

    A listing eBay is holding at zero because it sold is put back at the quantity it was published with, while the source still has stock. What eBay actually holds is read first, so only listings genuinely at zero are touched.

  4. Amazon stops answering

    Five listings in a row that read fine last time and fail now is a bot wall, not five bad products. The run gives up there, leaves the rest of the list untouched, says so on screen and in a notification, and tries again in thirty minutes.

Only what changed is touched: a price move never resets the quantity, and a stock change never re-solves the price. Notifications name the product rather than its product code, and forty price moves arrive as one piece of news rather than forty.

Deliberate limits.

  • It does not buy the item for you.

    From an order, “Order on Amazon” opens the product page with the quantity set and fills the buyer’s address into the checkout form when it appears. Nothing presses Buy now — that stays your click — and nothing dispatches for you.

  • It does not guess a fee, or anything else.

    A missing price is asked for rather than invented. An item specific is suggested with the evidence it came from, and written only when you accept it. A category the ranking cannot answer is handed to eBay’s own suggestions rather than filled with the nearest thing.

  • It does not touch a listing you did not ask it to.

    Nothing publishes without a confirmation dialogue listing the title, price, quantity, condition, category, delivery, picture count and SKU. A new install starts in test mode, where a listing is validated and recorded but never reaches eBay, and the panel always says which of test, sandbox and production is in force.

  • It does not send your work anywhere.

    No analytics and no third-party calls. Nothing leaves the browser except to the server you deployed and to eBay, and the eBay tokens live on that server, encrypted, rather than in the extension.

Questions worth answering before you install.

Does this fit eBay’s dropshipping policy?

eBay allows dropshipping where you fulfil orders from a wholesale supplier. It does not allow listing an item and having it shipped to your buyer directly from another retailer or marketplace, and it can end listings or restrict an account for doing so. That is eBay’s rule, not ours, and it is worth reading in their own words before you list anything.

Margin is built for a seller who holds the stock and dispatches it themselves. It never places an order, never presses Buy now and never dispatches; the confirmation dialogue says, before you publish, that you hold this stock and will dispatch it yourself. Nothing in it tries to hide what a listing is from eBay. How you actually run your business is your responsibility.

Do I need to run a server?

To publish, yes. eBay’s credentials and your refresh token live on a small backend rather than in the browser, and you deploy it and point the extension at its address. It runs on Cloudflare Workers on the free plan, or on Node on your own machine.

To try it, no. A new install starts in test mode, which needs no backend at all: it reads pages, builds drafts, prices them and records them locally without ever calling eBay.

How exact is the fee?

It is an estimate, unavoidably: eBay works the real fee out when the sale exists and reports it per transaction afterwards. This is the figure a listing has to be priced against beforehand.

The rates come from each site’s own published table, per category, in four shapes eBay actually uses — a flat rate, a rate that steps above a threshold, a whole sale re-rated at one rate, and three or more tiered bands — plus the regulatory operating fee where a site charges one. eBay UK’s table was checked against real order statements from this seller’s own account, which is where the setting for the extra percentage an invoice shows on top came from: Promoted Listings and the like come out of the same sale and eBay does not publish them. Once a sale happens, the Orders tab shows what eBay actually charged.

Which eBay sites, and does the account type matter?

Fourteen: the United Kingdom, United States, Ireland, Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, Switzerland, Poland, Canada and Australia — each with its own currency, fee table and site id.

The account type matters enough to be asked of eBay and cached for a day: a private seller on eBay UK pays no final value fee at all, while a private seller on eBay US pays what a business pays. An account eBay says nothing about is treated as a business, because quoting no fee to somebody who will be charged one is the expensive way to be wrong.

Does it use the Amazon API?

No — not the Product Advertising API and not the Creators API. Pages are read in your own browser under your own session, which is also the only thing that works: Amazon serves a datacentre address a captcha rather than a product page.
